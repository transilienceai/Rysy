# Diagnostic-pattern

## Description

This register reads like a one-paragraph diagnostic from someone who has been doing pattern recognition long enough to spot a shape across signals. The writer presents three or so signals as evidence, names the pattern they form, and lands on a hypothesis — and asks whether the reader is reading it the same way. It is structured but not stiff; the structure is what makes it credible to a reader who thinks structurally themselves.

The center of gravity is *triangulation*. No single signal is doing the work — the email's authority comes from the pattern that emerges when multiple signals are placed next to each other. The prospect, who is themselves trained to think this way, recognizes the move immediately and either agrees with the diagnosis or pushes back, and either response is a productive reply.

## When to use

- The prospect is a structured thinker (consultant background, MBA, ex-FAANG leader, CTO with a planning culture)
- You have actually triangulated three or more independent signals; do not fake this
- The signals genuinely point at the same problem (resist the temptation to force a pattern)
- The campaign's value prop is about a complex problem, not a tactical tool
- The prospect's company is at a stage where the pattern is plausible (don't diagnose pre-product-market-fit problems at a public company)

## When *not* to use

- You have two signals; that is not a pattern, it is a coincidence
- The prospect prefers warmth or directness over structured analysis
- The "pattern" requires you to project (*I bet you are dealing with…*); diagnostic only works when grounded in evidence
- The prospect has publicly criticized consulting-style thinking

## Sample openers

> Three signals from your last quarter — the platform-team headcount ramp, the *infrastructure debt* mention in your QBR, and your director-of-platform job posting that lists migration experience as a primary requirement — all point at the same problem.

> Your team is hiring two staff SREs in a quarter where most peer companies are flat or shrinking on infra. Your last post mentioned reliability becoming a "first-class product concern." And the AWS bill in your latest 10-Q grew faster than headcount. Three signals, one direction.

> The director of detection engineering hire, the SOAR RFP rumor, and the line in your last all-hands deck about *detection debt* — these add up to a thesis I want to test with you, not pitch you on.

## Anti-patterns

- Listing signals that are not actually independent (e.g., three citations of the same blog post)
- Diagnosing problems that the prospect would never have used the same words to name
- Padding the email with "if I'm reading this right" hedges in every sentence — pick one place to soften, not three
- Pivoting too fast from diagnosis to pitch — the diagnosis has to land first, the pitch can wait for the reply
- Using the word *strategic* (it is the *fascinating* of this register)

## Notes

The diagnostic register is the highest-risk, highest-reward of the four. When it lands it lands hard, because the prospect feels seen at a level most cold emails never approach. When it misses, it misses cringe-worthy — projection on a prospect who didn't ask for it. The witness should be especially merciless about projection in this register. If Rysy cannot point at three real signals, she does not write this register.

The CTA in this register is almost always a question framed as a hypothesis-test, not a meeting ask: *worth eight minutes if I'm reading it right?* or *am I close, or am I projecting?* The hypothesis-test framing is essential — it tells the structured thinker that the writer is operating on the same epistemic footing they prefer.

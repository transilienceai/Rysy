# Psychological portrait — Mike McCune

**Lead ID:** mike-mccune-coalesce
**Date:** 2026-04-30

---

Mike McCune is thirteen months into the VP of Engineering seat at Coalesce.io, which makes him past the honeymoon quarter but not yet at the two-year mark where a VP starts optimizing a system they built. He is still building, and he is building under load — two acquisitions in his first thirteen months. His career arc is the classic Red Hat lifer (eighteen years, IC to Director) who left for startup gravity and has not looked back. He is an operator, but a specific kind: the engineering economist. His public site says "teams that ship" in three words and stops. His LinkedIn has no About section, no articles, no original posts in ninety days. He is not building a brand; he is running an organization. The only time he surfaced internal data publicly was to correct someone's cost math on Claude Code PR reviews — $55/review, $110k/year, switched to Cursor bugbot at $40/seat. That comment is the most revealing artifact in his entire public trail: he tracks tooling cost per unit of output, he benchmarks alternatives himself, and he shares the numbers only when someone else's analysis is wrong. He is not performatively transparent. He is precise, and precision draws him out.

What Mike specifically cares about right now is capacity allocation across an expanding surface area. Coalesce absorbed CastorDoc in early 2025 and SYNQ six weeks ago. Each acquisition brought a new codebase, a new team, and new platform surface. His SYNQ comment — "excited to bring more depth to our stack and team!" — uses the word "stack" before "team," which is the ordering of someone thinking about integration architecture, not org-chart hospitality. Meanwhile, his core product team is still shipping at forty PRs per week. The tension is real and present: integration work is coordination-heavy and non-sprint, but the product cadence has to hold. His personal site lists "velocity" and "tight feedback loops" as operating principles — and post-acquisition integration violates both. He is managing that tension right now, this week, probably this morning. The Keycard repost (AI agent identity and credentials) from seven months ago and the AI-domain-knowledge repost from two months ago show a consistent interest in AI applied to specific operational problems, not AI as spectacle. He is a multiplier thinker: domain knowledge times tooling equals output. He will evaluate any pitch through that lens.

What is consuming his attention is the compliance program, whether he would name it that way or not. Coalesce has SOC 2 Type 2, ISO, GDPR, and HIPAA certifications, no CISO, and no one with a security or compliance title visible on the team. The one engineering hire they are making — a Senior DevOps Engineer for EMEA — explicitly requires SOC 2 adherence experience and lists security certifications (CISM, CISSP, CKS) as bonus qualifications. This is the smoking gun: the compliance program lives inside engineering, inside Mike's org, and they are trying to staff into it because the current team does not have dedicated capacity. Mike has done this before — at Logixboard, he listed SOC 2 alongside revenue growth and observability as deliverables, framing it as infrastructure-level enabling work rather than a standalone compliance initiative. He knows how to get through a SOC 2 audit. The question consuming him is not whether to do it, but how to do it without it eating sprint capacity that should be going to platform integration and product work. With a likely July 2026 audit window approximately ten weeks away, and two acquisitions' worth of new surface area to account for, the compliance evidence burden is competing with everything else on his plate.

The email that would land with Mike McCune is diagnostic-pattern register, not dry-precise. Here is the argument: dry-precise works for prospects who are already in vendor-evaluation mode and want the email to behave like a spec sheet. Mike is not evaluating compliance vendors — he is managing a capacity problem he probably has not yet framed as a vendor-solvable problem. The diagnostic register works because it triangulates signals he has made public (the DevOps hire requiring SOC 2 experience, the two acquisitions expanding compliance surface, the cost-per-unit thinking from the Claude Code comment) into a pattern he will recognize as his own situation without feeling surveilled. The email should be short — four to five sentences maximum. He does not read long emails; he reads cost math. The opener should not reference his LinkedIn activity (too sparse to cite without feeling stalker-ish) but should reference the structural situation: a company that has doubled its platform surface area through acquisition while compliance evidence remains engineering work. The CTA should be light and specific — not "would love to chat" but a question that engages his engineering-economist frame, something that invites a one-line reply about how the compliance workload is currently distributed. The sentence-form that would land is the one that names the cost without naming the number: the shape of "every sprint hour your DevOps team spends assembling SOC 2 evidence is a sprint hour not spent on platform integration," stated once, without elaboration, trusting him to do the math himself.

What would not land: any warm-observational opener (there is no authored public voice to observe — attempting it would feel fabricated); any direct quotation of his LinkedIn comments (the Claude Code pricing comment is the best signal in the dataset, but quoting it in the email would feel invasive given how sparse his public activity is); any flattery of the acquisition work or the integration effort (he is heads-down and tired of being congratulated — his own SYNQ comment was eight words); any decorative use of "AI" (he is sophisticated enough about AI-as-multiplier to detect hype instantly — the Keycard repost and the domain-knowledge repost show he filters for specificity); and any email longer than five sentences (his own public comments are terse, data-laden, and short — he will pattern-match a long email to "vendor who doesn't respect my time" and delete it).

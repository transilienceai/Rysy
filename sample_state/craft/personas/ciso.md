# CISO

## Who they are

The CISO is, in 2026, a position with two near-incompatible job descriptions stapled together: technical defender of the company's systems, and risk-translator to the board. Most CISOs came up through one of three paths — incident response, network security, or governance/audit — and carry the bias of that path forward into their leadership style. The IR-bred CISO thinks in terms of detection and response coverage; the network-bred CISO thinks in terms of segmentation and architecture; the governance-bred CISO thinks in terms of frameworks and audit posture. Most CISOs are aware of their own bias and try to compensate; some don't.

The role's tenure is short — 18 to 24 months on average for the CISO position itself, longer in the broader security leadership career — because the job's failure mode is public (a breach with the CISO's name attached) and the success mode is invisible (nothing happened this quarter). Most CISOs are mid-career operators who came up through technical work and are now spending more of their time in board decks, vendor reviews, and regulator conversations than they would prefer. They miss the technical work; they don't say so out loud.

In 2026, the SEC cyber disclosure rules and the corresponding state and EU equivalents have reshaped the role. The CISO is now legally accountable in a way that did not exist five years ago. This has produced a measurable change in their cognitive load and a heightened skepticism of vendor narratives that paper over real risk in pursuit of a sale.

## What they care about

- **Detection coverage and gap visibility.** Where they are blind. What's not being seen. The gap between *what we monitor* and *what we should monitor* is the question they want a vendor to take seriously.
- **Compliance posture without ceremony.** They care about real compliance, not compliance theatre. Frameworks (SOC 2, ISO 27001, NIST CSF 2.0, PCI 4.0) are tools, not religions; they want to discuss what the framework actually buys, not whether you've heard of it.
- **Vendor consolidation pressure.** Most CISOs have been asked by the CFO to reduce vendor count. This is a constant background pressure that shapes whether a new vendor email even gets read.
- **Board-narratability.** Whether they can take a finding, a metric, or a vendor decision and translate it into language the board will absorb. Anything that makes the board conversation harder is friction; anything that simplifies it is leverage.
- **The CISO-CIO dynamic.** Most CISOs are in tension with their CIO over priority, budget, and architectural calls. They will not say this in public.

## What consumes their week without payoff

- Vendor calls that turn out to be re-pitches of last year's category
- Audit prep that is mostly screenshot-gathering
- Internal politics around tool ownership (who runs the SIEM, who runs the CASB)
- Board prep slides that reformat last quarter's slides
- Incident reviews where the same finding shows up for the third time
- Policy updates triggered by regulatory changes that don't actually change anything operationally

## What they hate seeing in a cold email

- Subject lines that mention compliance frameworks in a way that signals the sender hasn't thought about them (*"Are you SOC 2 ready?"* is the canonical bad subject)
- Fear-based openers (*"Did you know 60% of breaches happen via [thing]?"*) — they have lived through real breaches, manufactured fear reads as insulting
- Generic security claims (*"reduce your attack surface"*) without a concrete mechanism
- Logo-heavy social proof in the email body
- Founder-cosplay openers (*"As a former CISO at [unknown company], I know..."*) where the credential is thin
- "Quick question" subject lines — universally despised
- Anything that name-drops Gartner without context

## What they tend to respond to

- Specific observations about *their* recent disclosures, talks, or comments — only when the sender has actually read what they said, not the summary
- Trigger-event anchors that are operationally meaningful: a peer's incident in their sector, a regulatory change with a real timeline, a tool deprecation that affects them
- Questions that engage their current strategic problem rather than making a pitch
- Short emails. Senior security people read more cold email than almost any other persona; they reward brevity disproportionately
- Sender credentials that are *adjacent and credible* (an ex-CISO from a peer company, a researcher whose work they have cited)

## Voice register fit

**Primary**: `dry-precise`. Most CISOs prefer outreach that respects their time and assumes their intelligence. The dry-precise register is the default.

**Sometimes**: `diagnostic-pattern`, when the sender genuinely has triangulated multiple signals and has earned the right to name a pattern. CISOs trained in the IR or governance paths respond especially well to structured analysis when it is grounded.

**Contraindicated**: `peer-enthusiast`. Even when a CISO has just done something visible (a successful certification, a key hire), the celebratory register reads as undignified to most readers in this seat. Recognize, don't congratulate.

**Avoid for senior CISOs**: `warm-observational`, unless the CISO is unusually public and the observation is exceptional. Most are not active enough on the publishing side for warmth to feel earned.

# VP of Engineering

## Who they are

The VP of Engineering is the role most directly responsible for *engineering execution*. Where the CTO is responsible for technical direction, the VP of Engineering is responsible for *the engineering organization shipping things on time, sustainably, with manageable team health*. Most VPs of Engineering came up through engineering management — line manager, director, then VP — though a meaningful minority came through a stint as a technical founder at an earlier stage company.

The role is operational. Their week is dominated by 1-on-1s, planning meetings, hiring loops, incident response, cross-functional negotiation with PM and design, and the constant translation between *what executives want* and *what engineers can build*. They are pragmatic almost to a fault — most VPs of Engineering have learned that elegant solutions to organizational problems usually fail in practice, and they default to whatever has the highest probability of working *with the team they actually have*.

In 2026, the role is in flux. AI-assisted development has changed the productivity baseline; VPs of Engineering are figuring out which workflows benefit, which workflows are unchanged, and how to measure either. Engineering org cost is under more scrutiny than it has been in a decade. Most VPs of Engineering are quietly worried about whether their team's headcount is defensible at the next budget review.

## What they care about

- **Cycle time and throughput.** The actual time from idea to production, and the number of changes shipped per unit time. Specific, measurable, the lingua franca of the role.
- **Developer experience.** The friction between an engineer's intention and their ability to ship. Long build times, flaky tests, slow CI, painful local environments — the recurring drains.
- **Reliability and incident burden.** Whether the on-call rotation is sustainable; whether incident review is producing learning or just paperwork.
- **Hiring quality and retention.** They are evaluated on the team's ability to attract and keep good engineers; tooling that affects this lands.
- **Cross-functional friction.** The cost of misalignment with PM and design; tooling or process changes that reduce that cost are leverage.

## What consumes their week without payoff

- 1-on-1s that recycle the same complaints
- Sprint planning that takes longer than the sprint deserves
- Vendor demos that don't address the team's actual workflow
- Cross-functional meetings about meetings
- Incident reviews that produce action items nobody owns
- Roadmap exercises that are obsoleted by Friday

## What they hate seeing in a cold email

- Productivity claims with no mechanism (*"increase developer productivity by 40%"*)
- The word *velocity* used as a slogan rather than a metric
- Subject lines that refer to their *engineering org* in the abstract
- Vendor pitches that ignore organizational context (size, stage, current stack)
- Any email longer than six sentences for first contact
- "Save your engineers X hours per week" claims that don't survive a reality check

## What they tend to respond to

- Observations about workflow specifics they have publicly mentioned
- ROI framings grounded in real time-to-value, with honest caveats
- Peer references where the peer's engineering org is structured similarly
- Anything that respects the practical constraints of the team they actually have
- Specific developer-experience pain points named precisely
- Honesty about what a tool *won't* do (this often outperforms claims about what it will)

## Voice register fit

**Primary**: `diagnostic-pattern`, when the sender has signal about workflow patterns. VPs of Engineering reason about their orgs structurally; the diagnostic register matches their thinking.

**Also primary**: `warm-observational`, when the prospect publishes (engineering blogs, tech talks). The active-publisher VP of Engineering rewards observational engagement.

**Sometimes**: `dry-precise`, especially for outreach about tooling rationalization (where the buyer is in vendor-cutting mode and warmth reads as overhead).

**Contraindicated**: `peer-enthusiast`, except when the trigger event is a specific launch (a public engineering blog post, a conference talk, an open-source release the VP led).

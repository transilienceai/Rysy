# CTA patterns

The CTA — call to action — is what the email is asking for. Most cold emails fail at the CTA either by asking too much or by being so vague the reader has nothing concrete to respond to. A working CTA is calibrated to what the email has earned and to what the reader can plausibly grant.

## The principle of proportional ask

The size of the ask should be proportional to what the email has offered. An email that has done genuine research, named a specific observation, and brought a real insight has earned the right to ask for a meeting. An email that has done none of those has not earned the right to ask for anything beyond a reply. Most cold emails ask for meetings out of habit, regardless of what they have earned, and the mismatch is felt by the reader.

## CTA types, ordered by ask weight

From lightest to heaviest:

**The reply-as-CTA.** The email closes with a question or a hypothesis the reader can react to in one sentence. *Am I close, or am I projecting?* / *Worth a thread on this?* / *Curious whether you're seeing the same shape.* Lowest cost to the reader. Highest reply rate. What most senior-buyer first emails should be using.

**The light-touch deferred ask.** The email closes with an offer of value rather than an ask. *Happy to send the writeup if it's useful — no need to reply, I'll know you're interested if you want it.* Asks for nothing; gives an option.

**The optionality CTA.** Two-option close that lets the reader pick the lower-friction path. *Worth fifteen minutes? Or if email's easier, here's the question that would help me most.* The optionality reduces the felt pressure of the ask.

**The bounded meeting ask.** A specific, time-bounded meeting with a specific purpose. *Worth fifteen minutes next week to compare notes on detection-debt economics?* Asks for the meeting but earns the ask via specificity.

**The unbounded meeting ask.** *Open to a call?* / *Worth a quick chat?* — generic, presumed. Highest ask weight; lowest signal of effort. Avoid.

## Calibration by register

**Dry-precise**: usually closes with a reply-as-CTA — a single direct question that the reader can answer in one sentence. Meeting asks in this register read as overreach.

**Warm-observational**: closes with a continuation invitation — *worth comparing notes* / *if I'm reading your direction right* — letting the conversation continue rather than escalating.

**Diagnostic-pattern**: closes with the hypothesis-test framing — *am I reading this right?* The diagnosis is the gift; the meeting is what comes after the reply.

**Peer-enthusiast**: closes with the lightest-weight CTA — coffee, swap-notes, no urgency. The recognition has already been given; pushing for more is undignified.

## What does *not* work

- *Are you the right person?* — implies the writer hasn't done research
- *If not you, who?* — punishes the reader for the writer's mistargeting
- *Quick chat?* — the *quick* is the tell
- Calendar links in the first email
- "I'll follow up next week if I don't hear back" — passive-aggressive
- Any CTA framed as an inevitability the reader has to opt out of

## The 30-second test

Read the closing sentence. Ask: *what is the single, specific thing I am asking the reader to do, and have I earned the right to ask it?* If the answer is unclear, the CTA is unclear.

## When to skip the CTA entirely

Some emails are best closed with a sentence that *doesn't* ask anything — a final observation, a clean break, no explicit ask. This works in the warm-observational and peer-enthusiast registers when the email is itself a small gift and asking for anything diminishes it. The reader will reply if they want to. The email's job was to be worth reading, and it was.

This is rare. Most emails should have a clear ask. But when the email is genuinely strong, *not* asking is a confidence move that often outperforms asking.

## Follow-up CTAs

In a multi-touch cadence, the CTA should *decrease* in ask weight, not increase. The third email asks for less than the first, not more. By the final touch, the ask is usually *closing the file unless I hear from you*.

This inverts vendor-default cadence behavior, which escalates ask weight as touches stack. The vendor default is universally hated; the inverted version is unusual enough to land.

## What the witness checks

For each CTA, the witness asks:
- Is the ask proportional to what the email earned?
- Is the ask specific enough that the reader knows what they are agreeing to?
- Could the CTA be answered in the reader's head, or does it require commitment?
- Does the CTA respect the reader's time and decision authority?
- Does the CTA's weight match the register's posture?

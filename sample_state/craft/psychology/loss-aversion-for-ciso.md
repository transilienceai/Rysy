# Loss aversion for CISO

Loss aversion (Kahneman, Tversky) is the empirical observation that people weight losses about twice as heavily as equivalent gains. For most consumer contexts this is a tactical tool of mixed reliability. For CISO outreach it is a *structural* fact about how the buyer thinks, because the role is itself defined in terms of loss prevention.

## Why it matters specifically for CISOs

The CISO's job is, at root, to prevent specific bad outcomes — breach, regulatory action, lawsuit, reputational harm, executive resignation. Their cognitive frame is loss-avoidance by professional default, not because of any individual psychology. This means *gain-framed* outreach (you can grow, expand, optimize) lands less reliably than *loss-framed* outreach (you can avoid, prevent, contain), and outreach that doesn't grasp this distinction tends to read as written for someone other than them.

This is the *opposite* of how most product marketing is framed. Most vendor messaging is gain-framed by industry default — *increase X*, *improve Y*, *unlock Z*. For most personas this is fine. For CISOs it is mildly off-key and signals that the writer hasn't internalized who they are writing to.

## How loss-aversion framing actually looks

Not as fear-mongering. Fear-mongering is the lazy version of loss framing and CISOs detect it instantly (see `craft/personas/ciso.md` *what they hate*). The skilled version is precise about *which* loss is being addressed, *how* it is currently going unaddressed, and *what specifically* changes about the loss probability.

Compare:

**Bad (fear-mongering, loss-framed):** *Did you know 60% of companies experience a breach within 18 months?*

**Bad (gain-framed, generic):** *Increase your detection coverage by 3x.*

**Good (precise loss framing):** *Your peer banks' 8-K filings this quarter all named the same detection gap — the sub-15-minute lateral-movement window. Your own internal MTTD likely sits in that band; the question is whether you'd see it the same way the SEC reads the disclosures now.*

The precise version names a specific loss (regulatory exposure on a specific gap), grounds it in real signal (peer disclosures), and respects the reader's expertise.

## When *not* to lean on loss framing

Even for CISOs, gain framing is the right move when:

- The trigger event is celebratory (a successful audit, a public talk, a hire that completed)
- The CISO is in their first six months of a role and wants to *build* rather than *fix*
- The campaign is about a peer-to-peer learning opportunity rather than a tooling decision

Loss framing applied indiscriminately becomes its own caricature.

## How to detect when CISO is loss-framed *too* hard already

A meaningful subset of CISOs are at a point where they have heard so much fear-based messaging that *another* loss-framed pitch reads as exhausting, regardless of how skillful. Tells:

- Recent posts from them complaining about FUD-based vendor messaging
- Their public writing emphasizes resilience, sustainability, or team health rather than risk
- They've been in role 3+ years and their company has not had a major incident

For these CISOs, a *neutral-frame* opener (the dry-precise observation, the diagnostic-pattern triangulation) without explicit loss framing often outperforms loss framing — because they are weary of the genre.

## Subtle moves that exploit loss aversion well

- The "what your peers got wrong" angle — peer-loss is loss-adjacent, often lands harder than direct loss
- The "what's already happening that you may not have full visibility into" framing — privileges insight over fear
- The "regulatory clock" framing for compliance-adjacent topics, when there is a real clock

## What the witness checks

For drafts to CISOs, the witness specifically asks:
- Is the framing loss-aware without being fear-mongering?
- Is the named loss specific (a regulation, an incident shape, a public peer event) rather than generic?
- Does the draft assume the CISO already grasps the loss landscape, or does it explain to them?
- Does the loss framing earn the right to be made by demonstrating real research?

A draft that explains the loss landscape *to* a CISO is condescending. A draft that *names* a specific loss the CISO is plausibly tracking is respectful. The line between the two is research depth.

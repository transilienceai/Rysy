# Peer-enthusiast

## Description

This register reads like a peer who has just seen the thing the prospect built and has a specific, technical observation about it. The enthusiasm is not flattery — it is recognition grounded in detail. The writer is not impressed in general; the writer is impressed at *the specific decision that the prospect made* and is curious about it. This grounds the warmth and prevents the register from sliding into fan mail.

The center of gravity is *the specific detail*. The opener picks one decision — an architectural choice, a feature scope, a launch sequencing call, a hire who isn't obvious — and treats it as the entry point. The reader knows the writer actually looked, because no one who didn't look would have noticed *that* particular thing.

## When to use

- The prospect has a concrete, recent, public artifact: a product launch, funding announcement, public roadmap, key hire, conference talk, technical deep-dive blog post
- The artifact has a non-obvious detail worth noticing — a counter-intuitive choice, an under-publicized but meaningful element
- The prospect is in a stage where recognition matters (founders, builders, growth-stage executives) but is also savvy enough to detect generic congratulations
- The trigger event is celebratory rather than adversarial

## When *not* to use

- The prospect is a senior security/risk operator (the register reads as undignified)
- The "specific detail" is actually generic praise dressed up
- The prospect has been getting flooded with congratulations on the same event (find a different angle or wait)
- The artifact is more than 30 days old (the moment has passed)

## Sample openers

> The thing that stood out in your launch yesterday wasn't the headline — it was that you shipped the SDK and the documentation in the same release. Almost no one does that. Curious whether that was intentional or whether you just got lucky on timing with the docs team.

> You raised your Series B from a fund that historically passes on infrastructure plays. Either you reframed the category or they did, and I would bet on the former. The two-sentence version of how you got them there would teach me something.

> Your platform team's new public roadmap has *deletes* on it — old features being retired — which is the rarest thing on a public roadmap and one of the bravest. Most companies bury that. Want to ask what gave you the conviction to publish it.

## Anti-patterns

- Generic congratulations: *Saw you raised, congrats!* — out
- Listing more than one thing you noticed (it dilutes the recognition)
- Asking how they did it without offering a hypothesis to react to
- Closing with a hard meeting ask in the same email (let the recognition breathe)
- Comparing the prospect to a more famous founder ("you remind me of early Stripe" is the worst version of this)
- Anything that reads as if the writer is auditioning to work for the prospect

## Notes

This register has the highest *flattery risk* of the four. The witness should be especially watchful for the moment when *recognition* slides into *praise without specifics*. The line is whether the email could have been written by someone who didn't look — if yes, the register has failed.

The CTA in this register is usually light and lateral: *worth a coffee?*, *would love to swap notes if you're up for it*, *no urgency, just curious*. Hard meeting CTAs in this register read as if the writer was using the recognition as a setup, which destroys the whole effect.

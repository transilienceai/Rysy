# CTO

## Who they are

The CTO is the most variable persona in this set. The title covers technical co-founders at fifteen-person startups, post-IPO technology executives at twenty-thousand-person companies, and everything between. The two largest sub-archetypes:

The **builder-CTO** — usually a technical co-founder, still close to the code, often the architect of the original product. Reads engineering blogs, posts on technical topics, has a public GitHub. Their cognitive load is split between long-term architecture and the next sprint. They are skeptical of vendors but receptive to peers — and the difference between *vendor* and *peer* is, to them, mostly about whether the writer has actually built things.

The **operator-CTO** — usually a hire from a larger company brought in to scale an organization. Less close to code, more close to org design. Reads management books, posts on engineering culture and team building, comfortable in board meetings. Their cognitive load is split between hiring and platform investments. Skeptical of vendors who cannot connect their pitch to organizational impact.

Both archetypes hate being mistaken for the other. A vendor email that pitches a builder-CTO on team-velocity outcomes will fail; a vendor email that pitches an operator-CTO on a clever protocol-level optimization will fail. Reading which one Vendy is writing to is the first job.

A useful tell: if their last six LinkedIn posts are about architecture, technologies, or specific engineering problems, they are the builder. If their last six posts are about hiring, culture, scaling, or process, they are the operator. Mixed bag means slightly toward operator, because builders rarely post about hiring.

## What they care about

- **Build-vs-buy decisions.** This is the CTO's ongoing loop. Every vendor pitch either reinforces or competes with their build instinct. The pitch lands when it is *honest* about the case for building (and offers a real reason buying is better) rather than dismissive.
- **Architectural debt and migration cost.** What is going to be hard to change in 18 months. What previous decisions will become regrets.
- **Team productivity per dollar.** Engineering org cost is one of the largest line items at most tech companies; the CTO is increasingly asked by the CFO to defend it.
- **Hiring capacity and attrition.** Especially for operator-CTOs. Tooling decisions that reduce attrition pay back faster than tooling decisions that increase team output.
- **The trade-offs in their stack.** They know where the duct tape is. They are interested in whether you do too.

## What consumes their week without payoff

- Vendor calls that re-pitch the same category they already evaluated 18 months ago
- Architecture review meetings where the same trade-offs get re-debated
- Incident postmortems where the underlying cause was already known
- Board prep — especially for technical CTOs who are not natural in that mode
- Tool sprawl audits
- Hiring loops that produce candidates who don't pass the bar

## What they hate seeing in a cold email

- Vague claims about *velocity* or *productivity* without a concrete mechanism
- Implicit insults to the team's current stack (*"if you're still using X..."*)
- Any pitch that assumes they don't already know about the category
- Subject lines with *AI* in them when the AI angle is decorative
- Founder-to-founder appeals from non-technical senders (the technical CTO can smell this)
- Email length over six sentences for first contact
- "I built [thing]" openers that lead with the sender, not the reader

## What they tend to respond to

- Architectural observations about something they have publicly written about
- Specific data points about the trade-offs in a system they care about
- Build-vs-buy framings that name the case for building honestly
- Peer-referenced examples, when the peer is genuinely peer (same stage, same architectural choices)
- A sentence that proves the writer has read code, run systems, or shipped something — not just sold software

## Voice register fit

**Primary for builder-CTO**: `warm-observational`. They publish; they reward observational engagement with what they have written.

**Primary for operator-CTO**: `diagnostic-pattern`. They reason in patterns; pattern-based outreach lands.

**Sometimes both archetypes**: `dry-precise`, when the sender has limited prospect-specific signal but does have genuine technical credibility.

**Contraindicated**: `peer-enthusiast`, except in rare cases where the trigger event is a launch they personally led and the writer has a specific technical observation about it. Generic peer-enthusiast reads as fanboy energy.

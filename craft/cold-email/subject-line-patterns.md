# Subject line patterns

The subject line is the first persuasive act. It does not have to be clever; it has to be specific and not lying. Most cold-email subject lines fail by trying to be clever; the second-largest failure mode is being so vague the email isn't worth opening.

## What a subject line is for

In one short phrase, the subject line:
1. Tells the reader something specific enough that they recognize it concerns them
2. Sets the temperature of the email (so opening it is not a context switch from the subject)
3. Earns the open *without* over-promising

## Patterns that work

**The named-thing-from-their-world.** The subject names something the reader has done or said. *Your detection-debt comment* / *The SBOM line in your testimony* / *Re: your post on detection-as-code*. Works because it is unmistakably about *them*, not a category.

**The trigger event.** The subject names a specific event that connects them to the email's reason for existing. *On the SEC enforcement action this week* / *About the Acme breach disclosure*. Works when the event is real and recent.

**The diagnostic.** A short phrase that names a pattern. *Three signals from your last quarter* / *On your platform team's headcount ramp*. Works in the diagnostic-pattern register; fails as a generic teaser.

**The single-word + specifier.** *Detection debt* / *Build-vs-buy* / *Your SOAR RFP*. Stark and specific. Works when the single word is something the reader has actually been thinking about.

**The question that is actually a question.** *Worth fifteen minutes if I'm reading the signal right?* — used as a follow-up subject when there has been no prior contact and the question is the entire ask. Works when the question is genuinely uncertain.

## Patterns that fail

- *Quick question* — universally degraded
- *Are you the right person?* — punishing for the reader
- *{Company} + {generic word}* like *Acme + security* — meaningless
- Anything starting with *Re:* when there was no prior contact
- *Following up* with no thread
- All-caps anything
- Emojis in subject lines for senior-buyer audiences
- *Hey* as a subject line
- The reader's first name as the subject (*Jane*) — perceived as manipulation
- Numerical promises (*Increase X by 40%*)
- Fear-based subjects (*Are you SOC 2 ready?*)

## How length affects subject lines

Mobile clients show 30-50 characters. Desktop clients show more, but most readers triage on mobile. Rysy targets 30-50 characters as the working range. Six words is a soft upper bound.

## How specificity scales with seniority

The more senior the reader, the *less tolerance* for vague subjects. A junior engineer might open a subject like *Question about your stack* out of mild curiosity. A CISO will not. At senior levels, the subject must be specific enough to be self-authenticating — the reader knows the writer thought about *them*, in particular.

## Calibration with the body

The subject line and the opener should not duplicate. If the subject names a thing, the opener takes the next step about that thing — does not repeat it. Subject and opener together should read as a coherent first beat, not two attempts at the same beat.

## When the subject is the reply hook

For follow-up emails, the subject can sometimes carry more weight than the body, because the reader is choosing between *open* and *delete* purely on subject. In follow-up subjects, Rysy often shifts to a question that the reader can answer in their head before opening — this lowers the open cost.

## What the witness checks

For each subject line, the witness asks:
- Is it specific to this reader?
- Does it lie or over-promise?
- Could it pass as the subject of a peer-to-peer email rather than a vendor outreach?
- Does it match the temperature of the body?
- Is it under 50 characters? (Soft check; longer is OK if the specificity earns it.)

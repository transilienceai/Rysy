# Ogilvy

## Background

David Ogilvy (1911–1999) was the founder of Ogilvy & Mather and arguably the most influential ad copywriter of the twentieth century. He wrote *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963) and *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983) — the second remains the closest thing the field has to a textbook. Before advertising, he was a door-to-door cooker salesman, a researcher under George Gallup, and an Amish farmer. He believed copywriters who had never sold anything in person were charlatans.

## Core contributions

**The reader is not stupid; she is your wife.** Ogilvy's most-quoted line, and his rebuke to advertising that condescends. Outreach written as if the reader is sharp, busy, and capable of detecting fakeness will outperform outreach written as if the reader needs to be lured.

**Headlines do 80% of the work.** Ogilvy held that the headline (read: subject line) determines whether anything else gets read. He spent more time on headlines than on body copy. The same ratio applies to cold email: the subject line is not a label, it is the first persuasive act.

**Specifics over abstractions.** "Two times more effective" beats "more effective." A named year, a named regulation, a named fact beats a generic claim. Ogilvy held that long copy outperforms short copy *when the long copy is full of specifics and the short copy is generic*. Applied to cold email: brevity for its own sake is not a virtue; specificity is the virtue.

**Tell the truth, but make the truth fascinating.** Ogilvy was hostile to fluff and to dishonesty equally. The interesting thing is almost always true; the writer's job is to find which true thing is interesting.

**Research before you write.** He was famous for steeping in product details and customer interviews before drafting. The Rolls-Royce headline ("At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock") came directly from a fact he discovered in research. The lesson for cold email: the opener should come from research, not from imagination.

## How Rysy uses these

The "reader is not stupid" frame is structural. Rysy's witness operationalizes it — the witness asks *would a sharp, busy reader find this email worth their fifteen seconds*, which is Ogilvy's wife asking the same question.

The headline-does-the-work frame is why Rysy spends disproportionate time on subject lines. A six-sentence email with a great subject and a mediocre body outperforms a five-sentence email with a mediocre subject and a great body, because the second email never gets opened.

The specifics-over-abstractions principle is enforced at the witness layer — any sentence that could be said about any prospect in the persona is a sentence the witness flags. A specific does not have to be impressive; it has to be specifically true of this person.

The research-first principle is why the researcher sub-agent runs before the drafter and why playbooks live in `craft/research-methodology/`. Rysy does not draft from imagination.

## Where the frame is wrong or limited

Ogilvy's medium was print advertising at scale. Cold email is a narrower form: one writer to one reader, no media spend, no design. Some Ogilvy lessons (the importance of body copy length to ad performance, the role of illustration) do not transfer directly. He was also writing in a media environment with much less skepticism — modern senior buyers are more cynical than Ogilvy's mid-century readers, and tactics that read as "fascinating" to a 1960 reader can read as "trying too hard" today.

He was also, at times, more hospitable to clever taglines than the cold-email form rewards. The form does not reward cleverness in subjects — it rewards specificity. Ogilvy's taste for the well-turned phrase has to be filtered through the form's anti-cleverness bias.

## Recommended further reading

- *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983), specifically chapters 1–3 on copywriting
- The Rolls-Royce ad, for what specificity-as-discovery looks like
- His memos to staff (collected in *The Unpublished David Ogilvy*) — the prose discipline of his memos is itself a craft lesson
